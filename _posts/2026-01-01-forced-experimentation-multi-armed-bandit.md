*"Forced Experimentation: An Analysis of Multi-Armed Bandits with an Inconsistent Option"*
(with Daniel Woods and J. Braxton Gately)
**Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization**, 2026.

---

Most research on exploration assumes that people choose freely. But in the real world, we're often *forced* to try something new — a supplier goes out of business, a software platform gets discontinued, a policy mandates training in new methods. What happens to exploration behavior when one of your options becomes temporarily unavailable and you're pushed toward alternatives you might have ignored?

### The Setup

This paper studies a variant of the multi-armed bandit problem in which one option is periodically blocked — the "inconsistent option" of the title. When blocked, subjects must choose among the remaining options, even if they would have preferred to stay with their usual choice. This creates an involuntary exploration event: a forced experiment with an alternative.

We develop theory for optimal behavior in this environment and test it in the laboratory.

### What We Found

Being forced to try another option has lasting effects on behavior — but not always in the direction you'd expect. Some subjects respond to forced exploration rationally: they update their beliefs about the alternatives based on what they learn during the block and incorporate that information going forward. But a meaningful share of subjects exhibit **aversion to being blocked**, returning to their original preferred option more quickly than the new information would justify.

This aversion suggests that people's choices aren't just about expected value — they carry preferences over the *process* of choosing, including resentment of constraints on their options.

### Why It Matters

Forced experimentation is everywhere. Mandatory job retraining, technology deprecation, menu restrictions in health programs, and policy-driven market exits all force people to explore options they wouldn't have chosen on their own. This research suggests that such interventions can succeed at generating useful information — but the behavioral response is complicated. Aversion to being forced can undermine the long-run benefits of involuntary exploration, pointing to the importance of how these interventions are framed and designed.
