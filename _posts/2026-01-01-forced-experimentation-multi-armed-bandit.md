*"Forced Experimentation: An Analysis of Multi-Armed Bandits with an Inconsistent Option"*
(with Daniel Woods and J. Braxton Gately)
**Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization**, 2026.

---

I think there's something genuinely important about the difference between choosing to explore and being made to. This paper is about the second kind.

Most exploration research assumes free choice. But in the real world, people are regularly forced to try things they wouldn't have chosen: a supplier disappears, a platform gets discontinued, an employer mandates a new tool, a policy intervention pushes people toward unfamiliar options. The question this paper asks is: when you're blocked from your usual option and pushed toward an alternative, what happens?

The experimental design introduces an "inconsistent option" — one of the arms in a multi-armed bandit that is periodically made unavailable, forcing the subject to choose elsewhere. This generates involuntary exploration: you learn about the alternatives not because you sought them out, but because you had no choice.

Rationally, you should update on what you learn during the forced period and incorporate it going forward, possibly revising which option you consider best. And some subjects do exactly that. But a meaningful share exhibit something the paper calls *aversion to being blocked* — they return to their original preferred option more quickly than the new information justifies, as if the act of being forced away from something made them value it more, not less.

This has a real-world resonance I find hard to ignore. Mandatory training programs, forced technology migrations, policy-driven market exits — all of these generate involuntary exploration events. The research suggests these can produce useful learning, but the behavioral response is complicated. Resentment of the constraint can undermine the benefit of the information. That seems like something anyone designing such interventions should know going in.
