*"Innovation and Information Free-Riding: An Experimental Investigation of Collective Experimentation"*
(with Tim Cason and Yaroslav Rosokha)
Working Paper.

---

Discovery is often a public good. When a pharmaceutical company runs a clinical trial, the entire medical community learns something. When a startup tests a new business model, other firms observe the result. When one country implements a policy reform, the rest of the world watches. In each case, the costs of experimentation are borne privately, but the information generated is shared.

This creates a classic free-rider problem: **why explore when you can wait for someone else to do the costly learning?**

### The Setup

This paper studies collective experimentation in a controlled laboratory setting. Subjects face a bandit-style problem in which the risky option has a shared unknown quality — if one person learns that it's good, everyone benefits from that information. Agents simultaneously decide how much to explore, knowing that their findings will be revealed to the group.

We vary the information-sharing rules and group sizes to understand what drives free-riding behavior and whether institutions can mitigate it.

### What We Find

Free-riding on others' exploration is real and substantial. When information is freely shared, subjects explore significantly less than they would in a solo setting — waiting to see what others discover before committing to costly experimentation themselves.

The result is collective under-exploration: the group generates less total information than would be optimal, and welfare losses from missed discoveries can be large. The paper examines whether changes in how information is shared — delaying revelation, restricting access, or creating asymmetric information rights — can restore exploration incentives.

### Why It Matters

This research speaks directly to policy debates about R&D subsidies, open science, patent design, and international cooperation on public health. If sharing knowledge freely undermines the incentive to generate it, then the design of information-sharing institutions matters as much as the incentives to innovate themselves. The lab provides a clean test of these dynamics under controlled conditions.
