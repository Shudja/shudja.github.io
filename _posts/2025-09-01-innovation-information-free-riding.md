*"Innovation and Information Free-Riding: An Experimental Investigation of Collective Experimentation"*
(with Tim Cason and Yaroslav Rosokha)
Working Paper.

---

Here's a structure I find genuinely troubling: when the costs of learning something are private but the information produced is public, the incentive to be the one who does the learning dissolves. This paper studies that problem experimentally.

The setting is collective experimentation. Multiple agents face a bandit-style problem where the risky option has a shared unknown quality. If one agent explores it and learns it's good, everyone benefits. This is a classic public goods problem wrapped in an exploration problem — the information is nonrival and (under the experimental conditions) nonexcludable.

The prediction is that free-riding should occur: agents will wait for others to explore, hoping to benefit from whatever information the explorers generate. The experiment confirms this. When information is shared freely, subjects explore substantially less than they would acting alone. The group collectively under-discovers, and the welfare losses from missed innovations can be large.

I find this sobering when I think about its real-world analogs. Open science is supposed to accelerate progress by making research freely available. Patent systems are supposed to solve the opposite problem by letting innovators capture value. Clinical trial data is shared to help the whole field. R&D subsidies exist because private incentives to explore are below the social optimum.

Each of these institutions is, in some sense, grappling with the same underlying problem this paper studies. The experiment helps isolate exactly how strong the free-riding effect is and what information-sharing rules might restore exploration incentives. That's the kind of controlled evidence that's hard to get in the wild.
