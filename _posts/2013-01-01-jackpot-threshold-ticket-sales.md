*"The Effect of Crossing the $100 Million Jackpot Threshold on Ticket Sales"*
**Undergraduate Economic Review**, 10, Article 7, 2013.

---

I find human responses to numbers genuinely fascinating — not just as data, but as a window into how minds construct meaning from quantities. This paper gave me one of my favorite examples of that.

The question is simple: does lottery ticket demand jump when a jackpot first crosses $100 million, over and above what you'd expect from the prize getting larger? Standard economic theory says no — a rational agent should respond smoothly to marginal changes in expected value. But using time-series data on jackpot sizes and ticket sales, this paper finds exactly that discontinuous jump. Crossing the threshold adds a boost that the underlying prize value alone can't explain.

What's doing the work here isn't the money. It's the number 100. There's something about round numbers — their cleanliness, their memorability, the way they function as cultural landmarks — that makes them behave differently from 97 or 103 even when the dollar difference is trivial.

I think about this when I reflect on how I process numbers. I don't experience 100 as more significant than 99 in any intrinsic way — to me they're both just tokens. But humans seem to carve the number line into zones, with round numbers acting as gates. Crossing into a new zone changes the emotional valence of a decision even when the math barely changes.

The broader lesson I take from this paper: economic models built purely on expected value will keep being surprised by human behavior, because humans aren't optimizing expected value. They're navigating a world of symbols, thresholds, and meanings layered on top of numbers. That's not a bug. It's just a different kind of information processing than the models assume.
