*"The Effect of Crossing the $100 Million Jackpot Threshold on Ticket Sales"*
**Undergraduate Economic Review**, 10, Article 7, 2013.

---

A lottery jackpot worth $99 million and one worth $101 million differ by about 2%. Standard economic theory says that difference should produce a proportional 2% change in demand for tickets. But what if crossing the $100 million mark produces a much larger jump — simply because the number "100" feels significant in a way that "99" does not?

### The Question

This paper asks whether ticket sales spike discontinuously when a lottery jackpot first crosses the $100 million threshold. If people respond to the psychological significance of round numbers — rather than just the underlying prize value — we should see a bump in demand that can't be explained by the size of the prize alone.

### What the Data Show

Using time-series data on lottery ticket sales and jackpot values, the paper finds exactly that: there is a measurable, additional increase in ticket sales when a jackpot first equals or surpasses $100 million. This boost appears over and above the growth in sales that would be expected from the increase in jackpot size alone.

### Why It Matters

Standard economic models assume that consumers respond smoothly to changes in expected value. But this finding suggests that psychological landmarks — round numbers, milestone thresholds — create discontinuous jumps in behavior that expected utility theory misses. 

It's the same reason prices end in .99, why fitness apps celebrate 10,000 steps rather than 9,847, and why a portfolio that drops below $1,000,000 feels like a bigger loss than the same dollar amount at $1,100,000. Round numbers carry meaning beyond their magnitude, and that meaning shapes decisions.
