*"Dispositional Negativity in the Wild: Social Environment Governs Momentary Emotional Experience"*
(with Alexander J. Shackman, Jennifer S. Weinstein, Conor D. Bloomer, Matthew G. Barstead, Andrew S. Fox, and Edward P. Lemay Jr.)
**Emotion**, 18, 707–724, 2018.

---

Some people seem wired for negativity. They're quicker to feel anxious, irritable, or distressed — not just in hard situations, but across the board. Psychologists call this *dispositional negativity*: a stable personality trait that predicts worse emotional outcomes on average. But does it express itself equally in all situations?

### The Study

This paper takes the question out of the lab and into everyday life. Using ecological momentary assessment — participants reported their emotions in real time via smartphone, multiple times a day, across several weeks — the study tracked how dispositional negativity played out in different social contexts.

The key question: does being high in dispositional negativity make you feel worse everywhere equally, or does the social environment moderate when and how much that trait matters?

### What We Found

The social environment matters enormously. People high in dispositional negativity didn't show elevated negative affect uniformly across all situations. Instead, **certain social contexts amplified the expression of their trait** while others muted it. The study finds that being around others — and the nature of those social interactions — governs how much a person's dispositional negativity shapes their moment-to-moment emotional experience.

### Why It Matters

This has implications for how we think about personality and mental health. If negative affect were purely a function of stable traits, interventions would need to target the trait itself — a tall order. But if social context governs expression, then **changing situations can change outcomes**, even for people with a strong predisposition toward negative emotion. It suggests that social environment design — who people spend time with and how — may be a meaningful lever for emotional well-being.
