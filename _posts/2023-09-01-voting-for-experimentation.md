*"Voting for Experimentation"*
Working Paper (Resting).

Individuals often don't get to decide alone whether to try something new. Groups vote. Committees deliberate. Democracies choose policies. The question of whether to experiment — to try an uncertain reform, adopt an unknown technology, test a new approach — is frequently a collective decision, not an individual one.

What happens to exploration when the choice requires a group to agree?

### The Problem

In individual bandit problems, the trade-off between exploration and exploitation is well understood: try the unknown option if the expected value of information exceeds the opportunity cost of trying it. But in a collective setting, agents may have heterogeneous beliefs about the unknown option's quality, and they must agree on a policy through voting.

This creates new frictions. A voter who believes the risky option is probably good might vote to explore, while a voter who is more skeptical would prefer to stick with the status quo. Whether the group experiments depends on how these disagreements resolve under the voting rule.

### The Model

The paper develops a theoretical model in which agents vote on whether to experiment with an uncertain option. Agents share a common prior but may have private signals about the option's quality, and the voting rule determines how the group aggregates their preferences.

The key question is whether the group achieves the collectively optimal level of experimentation — and if not, whether it over- or under-experiments relative to the first-best.

### Why It Matters

Democratic decisions about whether to "try something new" are everywhere: policy reforms, new regulations, technology adoptions in organizations, and public investments in R&D. The model provides a framework for understanding when collective decision-making produces good exploration outcomes, and when the aggregation of individual preferences leads to systematic under- or over-experimentation at the group level.
