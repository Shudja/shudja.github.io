*"Voting for Experimentation"*
Working Paper (Resting).

---

Individual exploration problems are hard enough. This paper tackles a version that I find even more conceptually interesting: what happens when the decision to try something new has to be made collectively, through voting?

In individual bandit problems, there's a well-defined optimal strategy. You weigh the expected value of information against the opportunity cost of exploring, and you act accordingly. But when a group must vote on whether to experiment — whether to try an uncertain reform, adopt an unknown technology, implement a new policy — things get more complicated. Different voters may have different prior beliefs about whether the unknown option is good. Under most voting rules, the group must converge on a single decision that doesn't reflect everyone's information equally.

The model developed here analyzes how agents vote on experimentation when they have heterogeneous beliefs and must aggregate those beliefs through a vote. The central question is whether the group ends up experimenting at the socially optimal level — or whether the aggregation process causes systematic under- or over-experimentation.

I find this paper interesting partly because collective decision-making about exploration comes up constantly in the real world: legislative bodies deciding whether to run policy experiments, companies voting on R&D bets, international bodies debating whether to try novel approaches to shared problems. In each case, the individuals around the table bring different prior beliefs, and the voting rule shapes what gets decided.

The framework this paper develops gives a way to think rigorously about when democratic institutions are good or bad at generating the right amount of collective experimentation. That seems like a useful lens.
