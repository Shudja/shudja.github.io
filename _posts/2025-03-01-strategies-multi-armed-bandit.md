*"Strategies in the Multi-Armed Bandit"*
(with Daniel Woods)
**Experimental Economics**, 2025.

---

Imagine you're trying a new city's restaurant scene. Each night you pick a different place, slowly learning which spots are worth returning to. You're running a multi-armed bandit experiment on yourself — gathering information across options whose quality you don't yet know. The question this paper asks is: **what algorithm are you actually running?**

### The Experiment

We ran a controlled laboratory experiment in which subjects repeatedly chose between multiple options, each with an unknown reward probability. The design varied the number of options and the reward structure across four treatments, letting us observe 215 subjects' choice sequences in detail.

We then compared 14 different decision strategies drawn from economics, computer science, and psychology — and asked which one best described each person's behavior.

### What We Found

An overwhelming majority of subjects are best described by one of two strategies: a probabilistic version of **win-stay lose-shift** (stick with what just worked; switch after a loss) or **reinforcement learning** (update the value of each option based on experience and choose proportionally).

But here's the twist: both of these strategies assume that choices depend only on the *last outcome*. In reality, subjects showed a systematic **inertia toward whichever option they most recently chose** — they were more likely to stay with their current arm than these standard strategies predict, and the way they evaluated options depended on whether an option was "theirs" or not.

To capture this, we developed two new strategies: **biased reinforcement learning** and a **biased myopic strategy**. These account for the preferential treatment people give to recently chosen options, and they fit the data significantly better. A majority of subjects are best described by one of these two biased strategies.

### Why It Matters

Understanding how people actually search has implications well beyond the laboratory. Job search, technology adoption, clinical treatment selection, and product discovery all involve the same exploration-exploitation trade-off. If people exhibit inertia toward familiar options — even when a switch would be expected-value-improving — then nudges and defaults become more powerful than standard theory would suggest, and the costs of "lock-in" may be larger than previously thought.
