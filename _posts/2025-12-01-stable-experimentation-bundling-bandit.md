*"Stable Experimentation? An Analysis of Multi-Armed Bandit Problems with Bundling"*
(with Jason Ralston and Daniel Woods)
Working Paper.

---

Standard bandit problems assume you can freely choose any option at any time. But in the real world, options often come bundled: buying one product locks you into a service plan, choosing one job closes off others, adopting one technology forecloses alternatives. When options are linked together, the exploration problem changes fundamentally.

This paper asks: **how should you explore when choosing one thing forces or forecloses another, and do people actually adjust their strategies accordingly?**

### The Setup

We analyze a multi-armed bandit in which options are bundled — selecting a particular arm forces the agent to also accept (or forgo) another arm in the same bundle. This constraint changes the information structure of the problem: now your choice reveals information not just about one option, but about a package of options, and the opportunity cost of exploration is different from the unconstrained case.

We develop theory for optimal behavior in this environment and test it in the laboratory.

### What We Find

Bundling significantly changes the incentives to explore, and subjects respond to this in a qualitatively correct direction — they adjust their exploration strategies when options are bundled versus unconstrained. However, subjects do not fully account for the informational consequences of bundling: they tend to **under-explore bundled options** relative to the optimal benchmark, apparently treating the bundle as a source of constraint rather than as an expanded information-gathering opportunity.

### Why It Matters

Bundling is pervasive in markets and institutions. Cable packages, software suites, insurance plans, employment contracts, and policy reforms often come as bundles. Understanding how bundling interacts with exploration incentives is relevant for firms designing product portfolios, policymakers crafting reform packages, and anyone trying to encourage adoption of new or risky options by linking them to more familiar ones.
