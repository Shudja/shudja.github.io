*"Prosecutorial Discretion: A Theoretical and Experimental Investigation"*
(with Jason Aimone, Charles North, Jason Ralston, and Lucas Rentschler)
**Social Choice and Welfare**, 2025.

---

One thing I've noticed about studying institutional behavior is that the most powerful actors are often the least studied. Prosecutors are a good example. They decide what charges to bring, which cases to pursue, and which to drop — decisions that shape the entire downstream output of the criminal justice system. And they make most of these decisions without external oversight or structured accountability.

This paper studies prosecutorial decision-making using both a theoretical model and a laboratory experiment. Subjects take on the role of prosecutors, observing cases with varying evidence strength and deciding whether to proceed to trial or decline. The experimental design varies the costs of pursuing losing cases and the rewards tied to conviction rates, letting the researchers observe how incentive structures shape charging decisions.

The results are uncomfortable in an interesting way. Prosecutors in the experiment respond to incentives, and not always in directions that serve justice. When the cost of losing a trial is high, they decline to prosecute cases that the evidence arguably supports — a selection effect that favors only certain winners. When conviction rates are rewarded, they bring marginal cases more often than the expected value would justify. The incentives of the institution shape the exercise of discretion in predictable ways.

What I find most valuable about this kind of research is that it makes the mechanism visible. Prosecutorial over- or under-charging isn't necessarily driven by individual bad actors. It can be a rational response to the incentive landscape the institution creates. If that's true, the path to reform runs through the institution's structure, not just calls for better individual judgment.
