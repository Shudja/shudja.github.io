*"Prosecutorial Discretion: A Theoretical and Experimental Investigation"*
(with Jason Aimone, Charles North, Jason Ralston, and Lucas Rentschler)
**Social Choice and Welfare**, 2025.

---

Prosecutors have enormous power. They decide whether to bring charges, what charges to file, and whether to offer plea deals. In most cases, there is no external check on these choices — they are made in the shadow of the law, guided by norms, incentives, and individual judgment. This paper asks: **what shapes those choices, and do they produce just outcomes?**

### The Setup

We combine a theoretical model of prosecutorial decision-making with a controlled laboratory experiment. In the experiment, subjects in the role of prosecutors observe cases with varying evidence strength and decide whether to proceed to trial or decline to prosecute. The design isolates how prosecutors respond to incentives — conviction rates, caseload pressure, and the costs of pursuing losing cases.

### What We Found

Prosecutors' choices are sensitive to institutional incentives in ways that don't always align with justice. When the costs of losing cases are high, subjects decline to prosecute cases that the evidence would arguably support — creating a **selection effect** that favors only slam-dunk cases. Conversely, when conviction rates are rewarded, subjects bring marginal cases to trial more often than is efficient.

The results also reveal that people in the prosecutor role don't simply maximize expected conviction probability. They exhibit **discretionary behavior** that departs from both the purely consequentialist benchmark and the purely rule-following benchmark, suggesting that individual judgment plays a non-trivial role even when incentives are clearly specified.

### Why It Matters

Prosecutors are one of the least visible but most powerful actors in the criminal justice system. This research helps explain why prosecutorial patterns — over-prosecution in some contexts, under-prosecution in others — may be a predictable response to incentive structures rather than random variation or pure bias. Redesigning those incentives, rather than simply calling for better judgment, may be a more effective path to reform.
