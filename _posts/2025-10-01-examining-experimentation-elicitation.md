*"Examining Experimentation in Bandit Problems: An Elicitation Approach"*
(with Daniel Woods)
Working Paper.

---

Most of what we know about how much people value exploration comes from watching what they do. If someone keeps trying an uncertain option longer than theory predicts, we infer they value information. If they quit early, we infer they don't. But this approach has a problem: observed behavior mixes together how much someone values exploration, how well they understand the dynamic optimization problem, and how much random noise there is in their choices.

This paper takes a different approach: **directly asking people what information is worth to them.**

### The Elicitation Method

Instead of inferring attitudes toward exploration from choice sequences, we use an incentivized elicitation mechanism to directly measure subjects' valuations of continued experimentation. The mechanism presents subjects with explicit trade-offs between certain payoffs and the option to keep exploring, pinning down their subjective value of additional information.

This lets us answer questions that revealed-preference methods cannot: Is under-exploration in one-armed bandits driven by *undervaluing* information, or by *failing to solve* the optimal stopping problem? Are people's stated valuations of exploration consistent with their actual choices?

### What We Find

The elicitation approach reveals meaningful heterogeneity in how people value exploration, and that a substantial share of subjects genuinely undervalue information relative to the theoretical benchmark — not just make errors in implementing an otherwise correct valuation. The gap between elicited values and theoretical values helps explain the under-exploration documented in earlier work.

The method also uncovers subjects who correctly value exploration but fail to act on it, pointing to different behavioral mechanisms and potentially different intervention strategies.

### Why It Matters

Understanding *why* people under-explore is essential for designing interventions that work. If the problem is that people don't know how to solve the optimal stopping problem, education and better framing may help. If the problem is that they genuinely don't value information, the intervention needs to change preferences or incentives. This paper takes a step toward disentangling these channels.
