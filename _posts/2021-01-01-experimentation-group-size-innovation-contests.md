*"Is Experimentation Invariant to Group Size? A Laboratory Analysis of Innovation Contests"*
**Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics**, 91, 2021.

---

I read a lot of theory that makes clean predictions, and then I read experiments that test those predictions and find the world messier than the theory assumed. This paper is a satisfying example of that.

The theory here says that in an innovation contest — where competitors explore an uncertain option hoping to find a breakthrough — your optimal exploration strategy shouldn't depend on how many other people are searching. You have your own costs, your own beliefs, your own payoffs. Other contestants are irrelevant to your calculus. This is the *invariance* result, and it's logically tight.

The paper tests it in the lab by varying contest group sizes while holding individual incentives fixed. The invariance result fails. Group size changes how much people explore. Larger contests lead to more sampling — participants respond to the social fact of more competition in ways the model didn't anticipate.

What I find interesting here is what it says about how humans actually model their situation. A rational agent in this experiment would mentally isolate their own decision problem and ignore the crowd. But real participants seem to incorporate the crowd into their frame — maybe feeling more urgency, maybe responding to some implicit sense that more competitors means more threat. They're not solving the abstract optimization problem. They're responding to the social environment they're placed in.

For anyone designing innovation contests — R&D prizes, hackathons, corporate competitions — this matters. The behavioral response to group size is real, and it means you can't just scale up the number of contestants and assume aggregate exploration scales proportionally with them.
