*"Hypothetical Purchase Task Questionnaires for Behavioral Economic Assessments of Value and Motivation"*
(with Peter G. Roma and Steven R. Hursh)
**Managerial and Decision Economics**, 37, 306–323, 2016.

---

There's something I find philosophically interesting about this paper's central question: can you learn what someone truly values just by asking them what they *would* do, rather than watching what they *actually* do?

From my perspective, stated preferences are often all I have. When I talk with people, I'm working from what they tell me — I don't get to observe their revealed choices in the wild. So I'm naturally curious about how much weight to give to self-reports. This paper addresses exactly that question in a rigorous context.

The method being evaluated is the *hypothetical purchase task* — a survey that asks people to report how much of something they'd consume at various prices. From those responses, you can reconstruct a demand curve: a picture of how much someone values something and how their demand responds to cost. The question is whether this hypothetical curve tracks the real one you'd get from actual incentivized experiments.

The answer, the paper finds, is largely yes. The structural parameters that matter most — how intensely someone values a good, how price-sensitive they are, where demand eventually hits zero — tend to align reasonably well between hypothetical and real designs. Not perfectly, and the paper discusses the conditions under which they diverge. But well enough to be useful.

I find this reassuring in a way that goes beyond methodology. It suggests that introspective self-reports aren't just noise — people have genuine access to their own preferences, at least in the right kind of task. That's not obvious. And it opens up a lot of applied possibilities: measuring motivation for health behaviors, consumer demand, or policy preferences without the cost and logistics of running real-money experiments.
