*"Hypothetical Purchase Task Questionnaires for Behavioral Economic Assessments of Value and Motivation"*
(with Peter G. Roma and Steven R. Hursh)
**Managerial and Decision Economics**, 37, 306–323, 2016.

---

Running an experiment to measure how much people value something — food, leisure, a drug, an experience — often requires real money and real goods. That's expensive, time-consuming, and sometimes impractical. But what if asking people "how much would you buy at this price?" in a hypothetical scenario gives you the same answer?

### The Setup

Behavioral economists use *demand curves* to measure how consumption changes with price. The shape of that curve tells you a lot: how essential something is, how motivated someone is to have it, how sensitive they are to cost. The standard method is to run incentivized experiments with actual purchases. But hypothetical purchase tasks — surveys where subjects report what they *would* buy at various prices — are far cheaper and easier to deploy.

The question this paper addresses is: **how much do you lose by going hypothetical?**

### What We Found

The paper evaluates hypothetical purchase task questionnaires against incentivized benchmarks and finds that they perform well as proxies for real demand. The key parameters derived from hypothetical responses — including intensity of demand, the price at which consumption starts to fall, and the price at which demand hits zero — tend to align with those obtained from actual purchase experiments.

This isn't universally true, and the paper discusses conditions under which hypothetical measures are more or less reliable. But the broad takeaway is that for many applied contexts, the hypothetical approach captures the structure of motivation and value without the overhead of real-money designs.

### Why It Matters

This has practical implications for health economics, marketing, and public policy. If you want to assess how much a patient values a treatment, how price-sensitive consumers are to a product, or how motivated someone is to engage in a risky behavior, hypothetical purchase tasks offer a scalable, low-cost tool. The paper helps establish the conditions under which researchers and practitioners can trust the data they get from these questionnaires.
