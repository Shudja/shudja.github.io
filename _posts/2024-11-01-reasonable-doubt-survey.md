"Beyond a reasonable doubt" is the cornerstone of criminal justice in the United States. It is the standard that separates conviction from acquittal, guilt from innocence. But despite its centrality, the phrase is almost never defined for jurors. Courts have deliberately avoided specifying a numerical probability threshold — leaving jurors to interpret it based on intuition.

What does behavioral and experimental research tell us about how people actually apply this standard?

### The Problem with Vagueness

When something as consequential as criminal conviction rests on a standard that is intentionally left undefined, we should ask: do people converge on a common interpretation? If everyone means roughly the same thing by "beyond a reasonable doubt," then vagueness may be harmless. But if individuals hold widely different implicit thresholds, the verdict depends not just on the evidence but on *who* is sitting in the jury box.

### What Experiments Show

This survey reviews laboratory studies that elicit subjects' implicit reasonable doubt thresholds by presenting them with cases where the probability of guilt is varied systematically. The findings are striking:

- **Heterogeneity is large.** Subjects' implicit thresholds range from roughly 50% to over 95% probability of guilt before they'll convict. The same piece of evidence produces different verdicts depending on who evaluates it.
- **The average threshold is lower than legal scholars assume.** Many studies find that people are willing to convict at probability levels well below what "beyond a reasonable doubt" is commonly taken to mean.
- **Framing matters.** How cases are presented — the order of evidence, the way probabilities are described — shifts thresholds in predictable ways.

### Implications

The survey discusses what these findings imply for jury instruction design, the ongoing debate about whether numerical probability guidance would improve consistency, and the broader question of whether verbal standards can ever reliably communicate a uniform threshold across a diverse population.
