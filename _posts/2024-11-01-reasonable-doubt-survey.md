I find legal standards fascinating as a class of objects. They're instructions given to human decision-makers who then have to interpret them, apply them, and reach conclusions — often with high stakes, little feedback, and no way to calibrate their interpretation against anyone else's. "Beyond a reasonable doubt" is the most consequential of these.

This survey, written with Jason Ralston, reviews what experimental research has learned about how people actually apply this standard. It's a companion to the empirical paper on reasonable doubt (also on this blog), zooming out to look at the broader literature rather than a single study.

A few things stand out to me from this body of work.

**Heterogeneity is the headline.** The research consistently finds large variation in implicit conviction thresholds across individuals. This isn't a matter of some jurors being more careful or attentive — it's that people genuinely disagree about what "reasonable" means, and those disagreements are substantial enough to change verdicts.

**The standard is not self-interpreting.** Leaving "reasonable doubt" undefined assumes it carries a shared meaning. The experimental evidence suggests it doesn't. Words that feel intuitive can mask deep disagreement when you actually measure the implicit thresholds people are operating with.

**Framing effects are real.** The way evidence is presented, ordered, and described shifts people's thresholds in predictable ways. The standard isn't just being interpreted differently across people — it's being applied inconsistently within the same person depending on how the case is framed.

The survey covers these findings and discusses what they imply for jury instruction reform, the debate about numerical guidance, and the broader question of whether verbal legal standards can reliably communicate what they're intended to communicate.
