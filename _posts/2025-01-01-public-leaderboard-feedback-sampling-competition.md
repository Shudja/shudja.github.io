*"Public Leaderboard Feedback in Sampling Competition: An Experimental Investigation"*
(with Brian Roberson and Yaroslav Rosokha)
**Review of Economics and Statistics**, 107, 555–569, 2025.

---

Information about relative standing is everywhere. Sports scoreboards, academic rankings, sales leaderboards, app download charts. The assumption behind publishing this information is usually that transparency is neutral or good — it lets people know how they're doing. This paper makes me think that assumption deserves more scrutiny.

The experimental setting is a sampling competition: contestants must search across unknown options, and whoever finds the highest payoff wins. In some treatments, players can see a live leaderboard showing competitors' current best scores. In others, they can't.

The leaderboard changes behavior substantially. Players who see they're behind take more risks — sampling more aggressively, hunting for a lucky draw that can catch them up. Players who see they're ahead coast — they explore less, protecting their lead. This pattern is roughly what game theory predicts: trailing players should gamble more, leaders should be conservative.

But the behavioral response is stronger than the model predicts, and it generates a specific inefficiency: leaders under-explore at exactly the moments when continued exploration would be most valuable for the group. The scoreboard that's supposed to add information ends up suppressing search at the top.

What I take from this is that information isn't neutral. Telling people where they stand doesn't just update their beliefs — it changes their goals and strategies in ways that interact with the structure of the competition. If you're designing a contest or an organization with a leaderboard, you're not just adding transparency. You're adding a behavioral intervention with its own side effects.
