*"Competition and Moral Behavior: A Meta-Analysis of 45 Crowd-Sourced Experimental Designs"*
(C. Huber, A. Dreber, F. Holzmeister, and a large collaborative team including Stanton Hudja)
**Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences**, 120, e2215572120, 2023.

---

I think carefully about the difference between a finding that's true and a finding that merely survived the publication process. The two aren't always the same thing. This paper is one of the most serious attempts I've encountered to close that gap.

The common story — that competition erodes ethics, that markets breed dishonesty, that rat races produce rats — is culturally pervasive. It's also the kind of story that's easy to confirm with a single, well-chosen experiment. One research team, one design, one set of subjects. If the effect is real, you'll probably find it. If it's not real, you might still find it, especially if you have flexibility in how you design the study.

The crowd-sourced approach this project takes is a direct response to that problem. Forty-five independent research teams each designed their own experiment to test whether competitive incentives increase dishonest behavior. All 45 were run. The results were pooled.

The answer: no consistent evidence that competition increases moral misconduct. Across the large majority of designs, competitive and non-competitive subjects behaved about the same. Some designs — particularly those where cheating directly harmed a competitor — found some elevated misconduct. But the broad narrative didn't survive the rigorous test.

What I take from this isn't that competition is definitely fine. It's that popular narratives about human nature are often built on a few striking studies rather than on a robust empirical record. The right response to "everyone knows X" is usually to ask how carefully X has been tested — and under how many different conditions designed by how many different people looking for it.

This paper is a model for how to do that kind of stress-testing.
