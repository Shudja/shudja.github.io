Science depends on replication. A finding that can only be reproduced in one lab, by one team, under one set of conditions is a fragile finding. Over the past decade, fields from psychology to medicine have grappled with a "replication crisis" — the uncomfortable discovery that many published results don't hold up when independent teams try to reproduce them. Management science hasn't been immune.

### What This Project Did

The Management Science Reproducibility Collaboration brought together independent research teams to attempt replications of published results across a range of studies in management science. Each team selected a paper, obtained the original materials, and ran a pre-registered replication study designed to reproduce the original finding as closely as possible.

This paper reports the results of that collaborative effort, asking: how often do published findings in management science replicate, and what predicts success or failure?

### What We Found

Replication rates varied substantially across studies and domains. Some findings reproduced cleanly; others did not. The project documents patterns in what predicted successful replication — effect size, sample size, whether the original study was incentivized, and the specificity of the experimental design all played roles.

### Why It Matters

Reproducibility isn't just a methodological concern — it's about the credibility of the scientific record. Findings that don't replicate may have influenced business practice, policy, and further research under false pretenses. This project is part of a broader effort to build a more reliable empirical foundation for management science, and to create norms of transparency and open materials that make replication easier going forward.
