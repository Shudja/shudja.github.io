*"Errors in the Pursuit of Justice: An Experimental Study of Type I and Type II Trade-offs"*
(with Jason Ralston, Siyu Wang, Jason Aimone, Lucas Rentschler, and Charles North)
Working Paper.

---

The legal system makes two kinds of mistakes. It sometimes convicts innocent people — a **Type I error** (false positive). And it sometimes acquits guilty ones — a **Type II error** (false negative). A central principle of Anglo-American law is that Type I errors are far worse: "better that ten guilty persons escape than that one innocent suffer." But how do actual decision-makers weigh these two types of errors when the trade-off is made explicit?

### The Experiment

This paper places subjects in the role of jurors and directly manipulates the stakes associated with each error type. By varying the payoff consequences of wrongful conviction versus wrongful acquittal, we can observe whether subjects' verdicts shift in response to the *relative cost* of the two error types.

The design generates clean variation in the socially optimal trade-off and lets us test whether people track it, ignore it, or respond asymmetrically.

### What We Find

Subjects are sensitive to the relative costs of judicial errors — they convict more when the cost of acquitting a guilty person is raised and less when the cost of convicting an innocent person is raised. But the response is **asymmetric**: people are less responsive to changes in Type I error costs than Type II error costs, suggesting a default bias toward conviction that is harder to move.

We also find substantial individual heterogeneity. Some subjects behave close to the cost-minimizing benchmark; others appear to ignore the trade-off entirely, applying a fixed implicit threshold regardless of the stated costs.

### Why It Matters

Criminal justice institutions are built on assumptions about how people respond to error-type trade-offs — through standards of proof, jury instructions, and appellate review. This research suggests those assumptions may not hold uniformly. If jurors are less responsive to the costs of wrongful conviction than to the costs of wrongful acquittal, then the legal system's presumption of innocence may need stronger institutional reinforcement than verbal instruction alone provides.
