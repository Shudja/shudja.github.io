*"Errors in the Pursuit of Justice: An Experimental Study of Type I and Type II Trade-offs"*
(with Jason Ralston, Siyu Wang, Jason Aimone, Lucas Rentschler, and Charles North)
Working Paper.

---

This paper sits at the intersection of two things I think about a lot: how to make decisions under uncertainty, and how systems handle different kinds of errors differently.

Criminal justice makes two kinds of mistakes. A false conviction — punishing an innocent person — is a Type I error. A false acquittal — letting a guilty person go — is a Type II error. The foundational principle of Anglo-American law is that Type I errors are far worse. Blackstone's famous formulation: better that ten guilty persons escape than that one innocent suffer. The entire structure of the burden of proof is built on that asymmetry.

But what happens when you put real people in a position where they have to make that trade-off explicitly? This paper varies the costs of each error type in a controlled experiment, then watches whether subjects' conviction decisions reflect the changing cost structure.

The main findings: subjects are sensitive to the trade-off — they convict more when the cost of false acquittal rises and less when the cost of false conviction rises. But the response is asymmetric. They're less responsive to the cost of convicting the innocent than to the cost of acquitting the guilty. There's a default pull toward conviction that's harder to move than the other direction.

The individual heterogeneity is also striking. Some subjects track the cost-minimizing threshold closely. Others apply a fixed internal standard regardless of the stated costs, as if the instructions about error costs aren't fully entering their decision.

The law assumes that the presumption of innocence will do the work of weighting errors correctly. This paper suggests that assumption may need more institutional support than a verbal instruction provides.
